Friday 4 September

Some waterfalls amaze with their flow, a wild power crashing onto the rock and drenching everything around. Others impress with their width, as if they could fill a lake on their own. Takakkaw Falls belongs to the first family: narrow at the top, it roars down in a column of white foam bouncing endlessly, like a pack of white wolves charging an invisible enemy. It's with this wonder at our backs that we set off on the twenty-two kilometres of the Iceline Trail, around the Yoho Valley and its glaciers. Tired of music to scare off bears, we've moved on to audiobooks. After all, the authorities recommend hiking in groups of four and making lots of noise; a voice coming out of our speaker seemed the ideal compromise to make up for there being only two of us. It's common knowledge that in the forest you must make your presence heard by every means to avoid an unfortunate face-to-face with a bear.

But not everyone seems to know it. After an hour's walking we catch up with a trio: an old man, a woman in her forties and a child. They barely respond to our hello and hurry to overtake us again after we've passed them. Their attitude strikes us as odd, but we pay little attention, absorbed in our story. We catch them again as the trail starts to climb, and sense a certain irritation. Finally the old man asks us to turn off our speaker: they came to the forest “to enjoy the peace of nature”.

Taken aback, we explain it's to signal our presence to bears. He replies condescendingly that clapping your hands and talking loudly is enough. We ask him what to do when the effort is too intense to talk; he has no answer. Voices rise, and we cut it short, telling him that every ranger and guide recommends music and that he'd do well to find out. Rather annoyed by this unpleasant encounter in such an idyllic setting, we can't even understand the logic of their irritation: a single voice from a speaker is just as if several of us were chatting, and in any case, walking much faster than them, the nuisance would have been short-lived. Luckily the beauty of the place soon chases the altercation from our minds. Out of the woods, we discover a green meadow criss-crossed by icy streams, then, at the top of the slope, the ridge at last. For two kilometres we walk over moraines edged by glaciers still covered in snow despite the summer, which spill into small alpine lakes of icy water. Just as well the sun is out, because temperatures can drop very low, even in summer.

After six hours of walking we're back at Takakkaw Falls and on the road again. Destination: Revelstoke, at the foot of the mountains, two hundred kilometres west. This stretch of the Trans-Canada is also among the country's most beautiful roads and crosses Rogers Pass, in Glacier National Park, at more than 1,300 metres. Shortly after, we move from Mountain to Pacific time: nine hours behind France now. In Revelstoke we slip into a campground to enjoy an incognito shower, then go into town for dinner. The pubs are packed for a crucial hockey game between the Vancouver Canucks and the Vegas Golden Knights, so we settle for a small bar with a pleasant shady terrace at the back. There we meet a friendly French couple who arrived in Canada at the end of March, just before the borders closed. They've come to Revelstoke for the ski season, due to start in October, and meanwhile work in one of the town's bakeries. They're not bakers, but Canada is one of those countries where you can start from nothing, and where motivation and reliability still count, whereas France only gives a chance to those with CVs as long as your arm.

Saturday 5 September

When you sleep in the car, the morning ritual is often the same. The first one awake waits silently for the other to open their eyes; after a few minutes lingering in the front seats, we return the vehicle to its original purpose: driving. The first steps out of the doors set the tone for the day, because the aches are still there, and you have to judge whether it's sensible to go and walk fifteen kilometres. We often brave the pain, fighting fire with fire, but today our spirits are flagging too. Too bad for the hike: Canada has activities for every taste!

We tell anyone who'll listen: you have to learn to free yourself from guidebooks. They're a base, not a bible, because they're very subjective, written by one or two people who probably don't share our expectations, tastes or budget. The Lonely Planet, for example, describes the road between Revelstoke and Vernon as ordinary and uninteresting. Yet as the Elantra's wheels glide over the morning's still-warm tarmac, some stretches dazzle us. The surrounding mountains keep a solemn majesty, and the Eagle River and then Mara Lake light up the whole valley.

We stop at a small beach and notice that, back in wealthier areas, the lakes are once again overrun with boats and jet skis. Quiet when we arrive mid-morning, the place is almost unbearable with roaring engines when we leave around 3 pm. Vernon is the next town on the road to Vancouver, and it'll be our stop for the night. It has only around forty thousand souls but sprawls endlessly around Swan and Kalamalka lakes. In Canada's smaller towns everyone has a house and a garden, not an apartment block in sight, but the charm the houses bring is swallowed by the huge boulevards that slice through the town. Vernon is no exception, and even though we spend an excellent evening on a pretty raised patio, it won't stay in our memories.

Sunday 6 September

Like the P'tit Train du Nord in Quebec, where an old railway line has become a long trail for walkers and cyclists, British Columbia has its railway relics. Along the Myra Canyon, for twelve kilometres, you get the thrill of crossing huge wooden trestle bridges typical of the architecture of the time. Canada is celebrating Labour Day this weekend, held on the first Monday of September as with the American neighbours, and plenty of cyclists and walkers are out with us this morning.

A mere anecdote, but every time we see someone cycling here we can't help giggling. Who knows why, but most Canadians, young and old, women and men, set their saddles very low, whereas the rule is that your leg should be almost straight when the pedal is at the bottom. It gives them a rather comical look.

After our ten kilometres along the canyon we drive down to Kelowna. The capital of the Okanagan Valley, one of the country's great wine regions, it's a big town ringed by hills, vineyards and orchards, with the huge advantage of lying on the gigantic Okanagan Lake and its many beaches. It's one of the province's big tourist destinations, hence the steady stream of cars this long weekend. The region is famous for its very hot, sunny summers, and it makes a change from the Rockies we left a few days ago: the thermometer easily passes thirty degrees.

We wanted to do a few tastings, as the wines here are excellent, but between the long weekend and the Covid measures, all the best estates have reached their visitor quotas for the day. We go for a more classic, and well-earned, programme: beach and idleness. The area lends itself to all sorts of water sports, paddleboarding, kayaking, water-skiing, jet skis, boats…, but like any self-respecting tourist destination, prices are much higher than elsewhere. Doing nothing on the beach suits us very well.

In the evening we have dinner on the terrace of a craft brewery. Mid-evening the wind suddenly picks up, and as we walk back to the car we're caught in a small dust storm in the middle of town. A few minutes later an alert arrives on Stéphane's phone: high winds over the region until morning… Thanks for the warning! With 120,000 people, Kelowna is one of the province's largest cities. The papers regularly mention the opioid crisis hitting this part of the country, and for the first time we see the damage: figures wandering the streets, eyes empty, talking to themselves, a scene we hadn't witnessed since Thunder Bay. It's always surprising in such a wealthy region, and we choose where to sleep tonight with a little more care than usual.
