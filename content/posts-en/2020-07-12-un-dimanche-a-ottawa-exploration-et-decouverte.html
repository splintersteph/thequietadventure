Sunday 12 July

Nine months in Montreal. Nine long months to live a thousand experiences, a thousand adventures, and to meet the most varied cast of characters. Since we left France four years earlier, we'd never stayed so long in one place, let alone in the same apartment. We'd begun to put down roots, and it isn't easy to cut them, turn them into a raft and set off on adventure again. Yet we must, if we want to keep living and discovering. So on this Sunday 12 July, at noon, we say goodbye to Charlène, our flatmate, stop by to hug Guewen one last time, and here we are on the motorway, heading for Ottawa. The road is much quieter than the one between Montreal and Toronto; then again, all government services have been running at half speed since the start of the Covid crisis.

For the capital draws most of its importance from its administrative role: it concentrates all the country's bureaucracy, embassies, Parliament, central bank, Senate… We'd seen the same set-up in Australia with Canberra. After an afternoon strolling around the main downtown sights, we have to admit it: the Montrealers didn't lie, Ottawa isn't a very endearing city. The architecture wavers between modernism and colonial heritage and, apart from Parliament Hill, the buildings are rarely beautiful. In the business district, charmless too, there's literally a Starbucks on every corner, so for the day the capital earns the nickname “Starbucks City”. We can't even give the Canadian Museum of History a chance, fascinating according to Charlène: it's closed, of course.

Our steps lead us from monument to monument; we read the plaques carefully and learn a little more about Canada's history. We also pass near the official residence of the Prime Minister, Justin Trudeau, invisible to ordinary folk like us. After four hours of walking, we head for the suburbs and our Airbnb. We're welcomed by an English-speaking couple in their forties, less friendly than the app has taught us to expect, and their adorable rescue pit bull. The house is big, but it's in indescribable chaos. The living room is buried under board games of every kind; we suspect the husband sells online, and it's certainly not the wife, since the badge in the hallway tells us she works for a government department.

We go for a run along the Rideau River and do some shopping on the way back. To our great surprise, we're nearly thrown out of the supermarket: we're not wearing masks. We didn't know they were compulsory in all public places in Ottawa, and we're in a fix because we don't have any. The manager lets us shop anyway but warns us that from tomorrow the police will start fining people. We wonder how we're supposed to get masks if every shop turns us away for not having one…

Monday 13 July

The Ottawa River runs north of the city and marks the border between Ontario, where Ottawa is, and Quebec. Over the bridge linking the two provinces, we enter the Outaouais, the Québécois pronunciation of Ottawa, as a little aside. The first town on this bank, Gatineau extends Ottawa in a much more industrial atmosphere. Before 2001 it was called Hull, and many people on both banks still call it that. The town is mostly home to French-speaking Quebecers, though there are plenty on the Ontario side too. This is where the Canadian Museum of History stands, the one we mentioned earlier, still closed, unfortunately.

A few kilometres further north begins Gatineau Park, thirty-six thousand hectares of forests, meadows and lakes, hugely popular in the region. In summer you can hike, cycle, swim, fish and kayak; in winter it's downhill skiing, cross-country skiing and snowshoeing. With all the Covid closures, this is our first real hike in months. We set off on the Wolf Trail, one of the toughest in the park. After an hour of steady climbing, we go from lookout to lookout, with magnificent views over the valley. There are bears in the park, and several signs remind us of it along the way. We're dying to see one, while wondering whether we'd react properly in an untimely encounter. We know the theory, but in real life? Hard to say!

After this good warm-up we picnic on the beach below, in the company of rather bold geese. Then we drive to another part of the park to visit the Mackenzie King Estate, named after Canada's tenth Prime Minister. For more than four decades William Lyon Mackenzie King spent most of his summers here, gradually enlarging and embellishing the site until he owned 231 hectares, which he bequeathed to the Canadian people. Restored in 2013, the cottages now house interactive exhibitions that take visitors back to his era. The place is so peaceful you'd gladly spend the day there.

Still, we get back in the car for Pink Lake, which, despite its name, isn't pink but turquoise green. It's named after the Pink family, Irish settlers who established themselves on this land in 1826. Its colour comes from a proliferation of microscopic algae: the sight is superb but harmful, because the vegetation gradually hogs the oxygen and suffocates the lake. Another oddity: its bottom and surface waters never mix. In most lakes the layers turn over completely every year, spreading nutrients and oxygen. Here, without that mixing, the bottom seven metres are starved of oxygen and no fish can survive. The only inhabitant of the depths is a prehistoric bacterium.

Before heading back, we pop into Walmart to fill the boot with supplies: tomorrow we're off camping for three days in Algonquin Provincial Park, three hours' drive west of Ottawa. We go on the Gatineau side, the Quebec side, where the law lets us in without a mask. That's the peculiarity of federal countries: the rules can change within two kilometres. We were used to it in Australia, and it's the same in Canada: each province has its own Premier, who decides for its territory.
