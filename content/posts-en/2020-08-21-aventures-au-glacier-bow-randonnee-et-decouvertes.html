Friday 21 August

Our two new friends are already leaving when we open our eyes at 9:30. Less keen on hiking than us, they want to push on to Jasper to find a campsite. We take our time, have breakfast in the service station cafeteria, then backtrack for the hike to the Bow Glacier hut. Different from the previous days', it's more athletic, and at times you have to pick your way over huge piles of rock. The weather is gloomy, a mystical mist shrouds the peaks, and we climb cheerfully. To scare off bears you need music: we choose a playlist of French songs, perfectly in tune with the greyness. We keep a good pace, singing and clapping, and soon reach the foot of the glacier.

The Bow River, which flows through Calgary and runs for 587 kilometres, is born here, a mere trickle of icy water flowing from the huge blocks of ice a little higher up. The hut isn't far now, but the trail has vanished, and the last part looks more like climbing than walking. Sleet starts to fall: wisely, we turn back. We have no wish to be caught by a storm we're clearly not prepared for. No matter: it's not the destination that counts but the journey, and the way back offers grand landscapes.

Back at the car we take the Icefields Parkway north again towards Jasper. The mountains vie with one another in splendour, and besides the magnificent viewpoints along the road, one of the big attractions is the Athabasca Glacier, a tongue of the Columbia Icefield, which covers 325 square kilometres. A small exit, a bumpy track, and we're at the foot of the huge mass of ice. Along the path, dated markers show how far the glacier has retreated, hundreds of metres in a century, and it keeps losing several metres a year: a worrying sign of global warming, which some sceptics nonetheless choose to ignore. Huge clouds are approaching and the rain is already catching us, driven by the wind that sweeps these heights. We finish the drive in torrential rain, the low clouds hiding the mountains completely. We reach Jasper late in the evening, grab a bite in a bar dedicated to the Canadian military, then find a spot on a quiet street to sleep the sleep of the just.

Saturday 22 August

Here you have to be discreet when you sleep in your car. Jasper may be a decent-sized town, but it's part of the national park, and it's forbidden to spend the night anywhere but a hotel or a campground. The situation is bitingly ironic: the campgrounds are full or closed, the affordable hostels are snapped up by families, and the hotels, exorbitant, all display illuminated signs saying they have vacancies. But who is willing to pay four hundred dollars a night for a place barely better than a motel? In the name of protecting the environment, the tourism industry has locked up Canada's parks, with barely veiled hypocrisy. You're advised not to cook outdoors so as not to attract animals, but no one worries about fast-food outlets whose smell carries for kilometres. You're constantly reminded, rightly, to leave no trace on the trails, but they rush to build roads and chairlifts that tear through animal habitat.

The weather is gloomy, so we use it to see places that don't require hours of walking. The giant glaciers pour into the valley and carve the landscape with the force of their waters: canyons and waterfalls follow one another along the river. Some are barely developed, like Sunwapta Falls, where we spend a pleasant time on the forest trails. Others, like Athabasca Falls, are pure Disneyland, swamped with tourists in sliders posing in front of every rock. A sign points out that the flood of visitors is irreversibly damaging the surrounding nature. Then why not close these places? Because tourists might stop coming all the way to Jasper? We strongly suspect the question contains its own answer.

At the foot of the Mount Edith Cavell glacier, you wonder if it's really August: the thermometer reads one degree. Not so surprising, in fact, since this part of Alberta has a subarctic climate. We'd even brought our visit forward after reading that snow could fall from early September and temperatures then drop dangerously. The forecast predicts temperatures close to zero tonight: we hurriedly book a room in a motel outside the park, as we're not equipped to sleep in such cold. After a day of sightseeing we're not sorry to have a shower and a bed again, and we hope for a restorative night.
