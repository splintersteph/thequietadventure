Sunday 19 July

This morning Stéphane's phone is flooded with alerts from the Canadian weather app: a big storm is heading for the region, whatever you do, stay indoors. Afraid of being stranded by flooding, common on the roads in this part of Ontario, we decide to leave before the worst of the storm reaches Midland. No use: it arrives sooner than expected and we're right in it. The clouds pour torrents onto the road and the windscreen, we can't see fifty metres ahead, and Cassandre drives carefully. A good twenty minutes like that, until a “Lightning warning” notification reaches us… two minutes after we've seen a bolt strike less than a kilometre from our route. We let out a huge sigh of relief as we leave the deluge about fifty kilometres on, at the gates of Collingwood. We'd meant to spend the day on Wasaga Beach, the longest freshwater beach in the world, fourteen kilometres of sand, but the rain won't stop and we're reduced to staying in the car.

An hour later the rain finally stops and we can get out and explore. Collingwood is a small, rather charmless resort town on Georgian Bay. Lots of Indian tourists are strolling around, though we couldn't tell you why. The town has no particular atmosphere, isn't very pretty and mostly lives off its privileged location. Fifteen minutes' drive away is the small ski resort of Blue Mountain. It's a far cry from the Alps or the Andes, but it lets Ontarians ski without straying too far from the big cities in the south of the province, Toronto first among them. The sky has cleared nicely, and from the top of the resort there's a lovely view over the lakeshore and the hills around.

All of Ontario is now in phase 3 of reopening, and shops and public places are allowed to open again; in practice, everything stays shut. The cinema hasn't reopened, nor has the library. In every country we've travelled through, we spent dreary days indoors: a museum, a film, a reading session. These days there's nothing to do, so we use the Wi-Fi from the McDonald's car park. You can criticise the famous fast-food chain all you like; it's when you travel long on the road that you realise how useful it is, and every long-term traveller will tell you it's sometimes a lifesaver. Toilets, a sink to brush your teeth, Wi-Fi: you know in advance you'll find all that in these temples of capitalism, without even having to buy anything. The staff see so many people that they don't even notice you come in. Very handy in these Covid times, when almost every public building with toilets, sockets or water fountains is closed.

Watching the customers, we make the same surprising observation as in other English-speaking countries: McDonald's crowd is nothing like the one in France. At home the target is rather young; here lots of elderly people come to eat. It struck us in Australia, then New Zealand and the United States, and Canada is no exception. Maybe because the chain has been around much longer here, and its first customers are now grandparents? Or because of a deeper cultural difference? Either way, it isn't about income, because the area is wealthy: the huge houses, cottages and sometimes even mansions we pass bear witness to it.

Monday 20 July

In these Covid times you can't help detecting a touch of hypocrisy in the measures taken by Canadian municipalities. Public services are closed, but the municipal staff are very much there when it comes to making tourists pay… The sun is shining today, so we head back to Wasaga Beach, that famous longest freshwater beach in the world. A trail runs along it, and we plan to run there to enjoy the scenery. Two nasty surprises await at the car park: it isn't free, and it costs eighteen dollars a day! Let's recap: every free public place is closed, but the town finds a way to post an attendant at the entrance to the paying services. We explain to the young employee that we'll only stay a couple of hours and ask if we can pay by the hour. No: eighteen dollars or nothing. This young man clearly doesn't know the French well: we turn round. No way are we paying eighteen dollars for that. Barely a kilometre away, a street offers us free parking.

The path shared by cyclists and pedestrians is poorly marked, and we get lost several times over the ten kilometres. Sometimes the signs simply vanish and we find ourselves running across car parks or along the roadside. At eighteen dollars for parking, it would be wise to invest a little in signage… After the run we'd like to rest on the beach, but a very cool wind sweeps the bay and soon drives us off. No swim. After lunch, a short walk in the nearby hills, then around 4 pm we arrive at our Airbnb, a very pretty, tastefully decorated house with a splendid garden, in the small town of Thornbury, cute but rather deserted. We intend to make the most of it, because we won't see the comfort of a bed and a bathroom again for several days. The night will be calm and very restorative, and we needed it!
