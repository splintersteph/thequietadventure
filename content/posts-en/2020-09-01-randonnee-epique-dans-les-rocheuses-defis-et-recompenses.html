Monday 31 August

It's amazing how quickly you get attached to two little balls of fur. One last moment with Kalpana and James, and already it's time to say goodbye. Dogs are extremely sensitive animals, and they've understood perfectly what's happening. For half an hour Kenya, the greyhound, has been circling us, chattering her teeth vigorously, a sign of affection in her breed. Stéphane, who has grown very fond of her, is heavy-hearted at leaving her.

Without realising it we dawdle, and it's noon when we finally leave, promising our hosts we'll see them again one day, somewhere in this wide world. We head for Calgary, an inevitable retracing of our steps, then further south still, into the Kananaskis region, to Peter Lougheed Provincial Park and its breathtaking mountain ranges. We arrive around 4:30 pm and set off on what we think is a moderately difficult hike. Thanks to AllTrails, the app that lists hiking, running and mountain-bike trails all over the world, we knew from other hikers' comments that the first two kilometres were tough, but we weren't expecting such a wall. The top of the ridge is only four kilometres away, but the climb is sheer, and to complete the picture, the ground is dusty, made of small loose stones that roll underfoot with every step.

But as so often, the view more than repays the effort, and the mountains remind us why you have to climb so high to truly appreciate them. The eye plunges over the valley and its almost turquoise lakes; you can also see the hand of man, which has drawn roads by cutting the forest in long straight lines. The wind blows in strong gusts and we don't linger on the ridge, all the more so since we badly underestimated the trail and the slippery descent takes time if we want to avoid a fall. It's already 7 pm; after a quick wash in an icy stream we go hunting for a campsite.

In this park, outside Covid times, you can turn up at any campground and register on the spot by slipping the money into a little box. But the pandemic has reshaped the world, and on arrival we discover you now have to book online. Neither the park's official website nor the tourist office mentions it, and there's no signal in the area: what to do? The idea of settling in without paying crosses our minds, but to be on the safe side we go to the pitch where the manager is supposed to be. An old lady understands our predicament and lets us pay her directly. So we hand over twenty-nine dollars, a price unchanged despite the pandemic, the lack of showers and of toilets with running water. We have to make do with pit toilets with a pestilential smell. Some value for money. It's so cold we don't even get the tent out and stay warm in the car for another night of roughing it.

Tuesday 1 September

“Because in the end, you won't remember the time you spent working in the office or mowing your lawn. Climb that goddamn mountain.” This line, attributed to Jack London, sometimes surfaces in our minds when the climb gets too hard and our legs too heavy. Today, on Pocaterra Ridge, it takes a lot of willpower not to give up, the slope is so steep and long. As yesterday, the climb rises like a wall before our crestfallen faces, on unstable ground: torture for thighs and calves already aching. The first hour climbs gradually; in the second we move at an ant's pace, one foot after the other, and the groups ahead of and behind us go no faster. The suffering shows on every face. Luckily our low points don't coincide: if Cassandre rallied the troops yesterday, today Stéphane sets the tone and encourages her through the hardest part.

With London's line still at the back of our minds, we finally conquer this slope, and as always the mountain keeps its finest rewards for the bravest climbers. What a sense of achievement, having lunch facing the vastness of the Rockies! Our day wasn't supposed to go like this: we'd set off for Sarrail Ridge, but a sign caught us cold at the trailhead. It was closed because of several grizzlies and their cubs nearby. More cautious than reckless, we fell back on plan B: Pocaterra. And we were lucky enough to see by the roadside our first caribou since arriving in Canada! The detour wasn't wasted after all.

From a hike verging on mountaineering, the walk turns into a series of ups and downs along ridges whose tops are sometimes no wider than a gymnastics beam. We move carefully, all the more so as we have to deal with a powerful wind, the afternoon's unwelcome guest. In the Rockies you always have to make noise in the forest to avoid bears, and even if it goes against the idea of calm we associate with the mountains, you get used to it quickly. We've swapped music, a little tiresome, for general-knowledge podcasts: business and pleasure combined.

Logistically, not everything can be perfect. This hike is superb, but to do it in full you'd need two cars, one at each end, because the ridge is a ten-kilometre one-way trail. With only one car, we're forced to finish along the road to get back to our car park seven kilometres away. We walk along the hard shoulder, sticking out our thumbs at every car. That's forgetting rather quickly that we're in a very individualistic country, where picking up a hitchhiker is unthinkable, even more so with the coronavirus still haunting minds. So we cover the distance on foot, and never has a car seat felt so comfortable. Off to Canmore, the biggest town in the area, where we grab a bite in an unremarkable pub before discreetly parking in a quiet side street for the night.

Wednesday 2 September

When you're young you think you're invincible and pile on the effort. But the body knows how to remind you of its limits, and aching muscles are a good indicator. On waking, the muscle fatigue is such that we decide to rest all day. According to our apps, we walked nearly four hundred kilometres in August: that explains the weariness in our legs.

We find a little café where you can sit and stay a few hours, before calling our parents, who are spending the day together in the south of France. Their good mood is contagious, and we leave invigorated for the Smith-Dorrien scenic drive, a superb road between peaks and lakes, or so they say. Barely a kilometre on, it turns into a stony track in a sorry state, torture for our brand-new tyres. No way are we putting ourselves through forty minutes of that: too bad for the view, we turn round and go to cook at the sports centre, which has outdoor tables. The fierce wind is back, we're cold, and for the first time on the road trip we're both irritated at the same time. It takes nearly forty minutes to cook our poor pasta, which is cold as soon as it's drained! Mid-afternoon has come and gone, we won't be hiking today: we might as well carry on west. We find an Airbnb a few hours away, in the small town of Golden, British Columbia, and leave almost at once. The Rockies' climate is starting to test our patience.

Once again the road is superb, among the peaks and glaciers of Yoho National Park, which we plan to explore over the next two days. You can only marvel at these grand landscapes unfolding as you drive, and salute the engineering that built these roads. At 6 pm we arrive, get out of the car and, miracle, we're no longer cold! It's mild, and we can stroll around in T-shirts. After a well-earned bath we have dinner at one of the restaurants in this neat little town. When we get back, our hosts, sitting in their living room, strike up a conversation. They're two seasoned travellers in their sixties who have roamed many countries, and our experiences overlap when we talk about Australia, which they toured in a van thirty years ago!

This couple are among the many Canadian retirees who leave their country every winter for more exotic places. This year was no exception: they were relaxing in the Philippines when the coronavirus struck. The very strict Philippine government put the country in quarantine and cut all sea and air links. Our hosts were stuck for six weeks in their little hotel on the island of Boracay. There are worse places, admittedly, but the quarantine forbade going out at all, as in France, and the nearby beach didn't change much. Until one day, six weeks later, a plane chartered by the Canadian government came to pick up all the nationals stranded in the archipelago, for the modest sum of 2,500 dollars each, non-refundable of course. We talk about all sorts of things for more than an hour, then, as fatigue sets in, we excuse ourselves to enjoy a real bed.

Thursday 3 September

Yoho National Park is also in the Rockies, but on the British Columbia side, the country's westernmost province. In Cree, Yoho expresses awe and wonder, which does full justice to its landscapes, especially around Emerald Lake, one of the jewels of world tourism. That's where we go today, to admire the lake and stretch our legs on an eight-kilometre hike to the Emerald Basin.

English speakers have a wonderful expression, “nothing to write home about”, for something a bit ordinary. We won't go that far, because the Rockies stay fabulous in all circumstances, but this hike leaves us a little unsatisfied. First, its name is misleading: at the end there's no basin, just a trickle of water coming down from the glaciers. And most of the path runs through forest, which takes away much of its drama. Still, we're pleased with the effort, and end the day with the loop around the lake; the weather is gloomy, and the clouds give the place an ominous air.

Canada is full of small isolated towns, and it's in Field that we spend the evening. The railway and the highway are of course part of the picture, since these villages owe their existence to those two trans-Canadian arteries. We pick the furthest street of the dozen or so in the village and switch off the engine for an almost perfectly quiet night.
