Wednesday 19 August

The alarm goes at 7 and we struggle to surface. No dawdling, though: every passing minute brings its stream of cars full of holidaymakers come to admire Lake Louise. Type “Canada” into Google Images: chances are one of the first photos will be of this lake, so iconic is it. We plan a long hike of twenty kilometres and seven hours along the heights, with breathtaking views over the turquoise lake and the valley. The loop starts with a four-kilometre climb to the Lake Agnes Tea House, at 2,100 metres. Our speaker and bear spray won't be needed today: it's only 8 am, but tourists are already out in force. Most are very poorly shod, clearly not used to walking, and we overtake many of them, puffing up the four hundred metres of climbing.

After an hour's climb we discover this little high-altitude lake and its rustic tea house, already overrun at 9 in the morning! A queue stretches out, people crowd around the little chalet. What makes the place so popular that even tourists who never walk come up? Built in 1901 by the Canadian Pacific Railway as a shelter for hikers, it now offers more than a hundred teas from around the world, made with water from Lake Agnes, boiled first to kill any bacteria. The staff, mostly students on summer break, live on site, and all supplies come up on foot or on horseback. Its pastries are also highly spoken of.

We skip the tea and cakes and carry on, away from the crowd, round Lake Agnes and up to the Big Beehive lookout. The slope is steep and the heart races, but the climb only takes about twenty minutes, and there are already far fewer of us. The viewpoint takes in the turquoise waters of Lake Louise below, an almost unreal colour, and some naturally take the chance to get their best Instagram shots. We go down the far side of the ridge to join the Plain of Six Glaciers trail. Most hikers choose one or the other, Lake Agnes and its lookout or the glacier trail, but you can link the two for a big twenty-kilometre loop.

The path runs deep into the valley, to the glaciers whose meltwater feeds Lake Louise. It's the fine particles of rock it carries, the “rock flour”, that give the lake its turquoise colour. After seven hours of walking we're back on its shore and follow it to the car park. The lake isn't set up for swimming, and the water is so cold a dip would be torture. There's hardly anything around it anyway, apart from a kayak rental at ten times the Canadian average and a huge, very ugly luxury Fairmont hotel that completely spoils the site. How could the authorities allow such an eyesore beside such a wonder?

Back in the village we wash quickly by the river: no lingering dip, just enough to get clean. We also stop at the park campground, full for ages, to fill our water can. A frugal dinner, and we return to our little side street.

Thursday 20 August

Yesterday's 7 am alarm was painful; today's is worse. That's the price of Moraine Lake: its car park holds only about a hundred vehicles, first come, first served. At 5 the alarm rings and we set off without wasting a minute. Still half asleep, Stéphane drives slowly up the mountain road that winds to the lake. It's pitch dark, and we even glimpse large deer at the forest's edge. When we arrive around 5:15, there are already plenty of cars, and we park almost at the back. We don't get out straight away: we fall asleep again at once and only resurface three hours later.

In normal times, those wanting to avoid a dawn start can take a free shuttle from the village centre. We understand it's suspended in these exceptional circumstances, but once again it's public services that are cut first: a private shuttle runs the same route for the modest sum of twenty-five dollars a head. Public health concerns clearly stop where the lure of profit begins. Moraine Lake is less crowded than its neighbour, but it has its share of tourists. We know that as soon as the trail climbs we'll find the calm of the forest again, so we head for Sentinel Pass, at 2,600 metres. Hike after hike, we notice there are always Quebecers around as soon as a trail gets physically tough. They struck us as a very active people; this only confirms it. Several are around us at the pass, also savouring the view of these extraordinary mountains. On the way down we detour via Eiffel Lake, a high lake ringed by peaks, without lingering, because the rain is starting. In the mountains, as we know, the weather is unpredictable and changes very fast.

At 3 pm, back by the lake, we have lunch: a tuna pasta salad made the day before. Then we go back to the village campground to wash in the facilities. Not being guests, we're not supposed to go in, but the lack of facilities for travellers in Canada pushes us into this small illegality, which amuses us greatly. Nothing very wicked, and we leave a quarter of an hour later. We leave Lake Louise by the slip road onto Highway 93, better known as the Icefields Parkway. This scenic road, one of the most beautiful in the world, winds through the heart of the Rockies for more than two hundred kilometres, across Banff and Jasper national parks.

Along this road to Jasper there are unstaffed campgrounds where you register yourself day by day, but the first two are full or closed because of Covid. After eighty kilometres we reach Saskatchewan River Crossing, a stop where two roads meet and the North Saskatchewan River flows past. Tired of searching, we decide to sleep in a space in the motel car park. But off to the side we notice a big gravel area and a young man busy around his van, a backpacker like us by the look of it. We ask him in English whether you're allowed to sleep there, and his accent jumps out at us: he's French. His name is Maël and he confirms you can stay parked here overnight for free. His girlfriend Cindy joins him, and we spend the evening chatting over beers and a basic campers' meal: flageolet beans, pasta and sardines. Both are from Nantes and have made more or less the same journeys as us in recent years, including a year in Australia: there's no shortage of things to talk about. We're in the middle of nowhere, surrounded by towering mountains, and only the cold and the dark bring this lovely evening to an end.
