Saturday 25 July

At last we're on Manitoulin Island, and the irony is that the seven-hundred-kilometre detour cost us less than the ferry, thanks to our thrifty Hyundai Elantra. We can finally enjoy the landscapes of the largest lake island in the world. A peaceful atmosphere reigns, and the locals all wear smiles. Life doesn't seem very hard for them: they live by the water and nearly all own a boat which, when it isn't sleeping in the driveway, spends most of its time sailing the clear waters of Lake Huron. We find Mindemoya's municipal beach, a simple strip of grass by the water, and enjoy its peace while wading in the shallows. The water is incredibly clear, and in the popular imagination it would sit more easily in Guadeloupe than in Ontario.

The place is wildly beautiful, and we take the chance to call our families. Because however lucky we are, living the chance of a lifetime, crossing Canada at our own pace, we miss our parents, loved ones and friends terribly. We spend several hours talking, since three conditions are met: signal, a free afternoon and enough sun for our solar panel. How good it is to share such a place with them and hear their voices! The little beach is so peaceful that we decide to spend the night here. Sometimes you have to know when to stay put and appreciate what's in front of you.

Sunday 26 July

Early in the morning we tackle Manitoulin's most popular hike, three hours along the top of the escarpment, with endless views over the island's contours and the lakes around. Then we take the wheel for several hours, with brief stops to fill up, buy food and fill our water can for the three days and nights of camping ahead. In theory the provincial parks we're visiting let you refill with water, but you can never be too careful, especially with water.

As we head west along the north shore of Lake Huron, the weather turns. Arriving in Sault Ste. Marie, a border town with Michigan, where Lake Superior meets Lake Huron, we stop at a café for a snack. Right then a downpour begins, which won't stop for an hour. Our stop for the day, Harmony Beach, a little beachside hamlet, is only twenty kilometres away, so we carry on. The rain has stopped, but a thick fog has settled, and winding between green hills drowned in mist, you'd think you were in Ireland.

Harmony Beach isn't really a village: more a string of holiday homes and a campground along Lake Superior, the largest freshwater lake on the planet. The weather keeps walkers away and the beach is almost empty. The sky is grey, but the air is extremely heavy: thirty-six degrees with the humidity and 97% humidity. We're a long way from the French climate; this permanent dampness takes us back to Colombia or Guadeloupe. Without a second thought we put on our swimsuits, and surprise, the water is warm! It must be around twenty-three degrees, astonishing when you know this part of the lake was frozen a few months ago. Unlike the beaches of the Bruce Peninsula, there are no rocks here: Harmony Beach has fine sand worthy of a seaside beach and water, once again, translucent. In fine weather it must be magnificent. Ontario's weather is really fickle these days, but that's normal in the Great Lakes region: heat and humidity rarely get on well. We wanted to camp on the beach, but the sand is soaked; we'll sleep in the car after all and put off wild camping until tomorrow.

Monday 27 July

“Oh, you're the smart one!” an old lady says to Cassandre as we pass her on a trail in Pancake Bay Provincial Park. She's praising Cass's good sense in wearing her mosquito head net. It makes her look like a beekeeper, but it does the job: keeping at bay the swarms of mosquitoes watching us like hungry people watching for their food delivery. Brush past a shrub or a fern and clouds of them rise, curious and ready to devour us. It's a constant in Ontario: trails are very badly marked. After a while we realise the signs were put up before the vegetation grew back, because they're often hidden by the towering Canadian trees. Pancake Bay, named for its bay and its pancake-shaped sandy beach, is no exception, and what was bound to happen one day happens: we get lost.

In the car park a sign offers three routes: out and back to the viewpoint over the bay, a five-kilometre loop and a longer one of eight. We choose that one, light of step and fresh of leg, before it's even 10 am. We're alone, the forest is silent, we keep a good pace. The path is wide and well laid out as long as it follows the first two loops. But as soon as we enter the third, the vegetation thickens to the point of sometimes hiding the way completely. We walk a long while almost through ferns before sighing with relief as we come out… only to find we're back at a spot we've already passed.

The app tells us: we've taken a wrong turn, without seeing a single sign. After a dozen countries explored, it's the first time we've got lost hiking, and our troubles aren't over, because we miss the trail again. Guided by Maps.Me, we finally find the tiny path that leads back into the forest. A hike that should have taken two hours takes more than three, and even the superb view from the lookout isn't enough to console us.

But the park is best known for its superb beach, which wouldn't look out of place in Australia, and we spend the rest of the afternoon there: rest, and a wash in the lake. We're entering less populated parts of Ontario, and free campsites are starting to multiply. WikiCamps points one out; after filling up and a bit of shopping, we go and inspect it. Verdict: perfect! Nestled on a little ridge above Lake Superior, it has room for several campers, and a couple are already there with a big converted 4x4. We pitch the tent and make dinner facing the sunset, whose colours keep changing as it slides behind the horizon. “No two sunsets are alike, you never tire of them,” goes a saying. We're starting to think it's true.
